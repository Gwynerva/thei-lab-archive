I grew up in Whitby, on the North Yorkshire coast, where the River Esk comes out between two cliffs into the North Sea. Everybody I've ever met has either been to Whitby or has always meant to go. This is where I'd take you.

## The East Side

Cross the swing bridge and turn left along Church Street, the old street under the East Cliff, all cobbles and jet shops and the smell of fudge. At the end are the :hint[199 steps]{note="Count them on the way up. Everyone gets a different number, and everyone is sure they're right."}, which take you up to St Mary's Church and the abbey. Go up at dusk, when the lamps come on and the whole harbour is laid out below you.

The abbey has been a ruin since Henry VIII, and it's the most beautiful ruin I know. The wind on the headland will try to take your hat. Let it have something else.

## The harbour

The river splits the town in two, and the swing bridge joins it up again, except when it swings open for a boat and everybody stops to watch. Two piers with lighthouses guard the harbour mouth. Walk out to the end of the West Pier on a calm day and look back at the town. On a rough day, don't: the waves come right over.

Halfway along the harbour on the west side is *Testwell's Chandlery*, Dad's shop: rope, paint, shackles, fishing tackle, advice nobody asked for, and the tide table behind the till. Go in and say hello. Tell him I sent you. He'll pretend not to be pleased.

## The West Side

Up the hill from the harbour is the West Cliff, with the whalebone arch framing the abbey across the water (everyone takes that photograph; take it anyway), Captain Cook's statue looking out to sea, and a long row of beach huts down on the sands.

## Further along

- **Saltwick Bay**, a mile south along the cliff path: Black Nab, the rock platform, the fossils, and the cliffs [I spent a year measuring](ref:project:whitby-erosion). Look at the tide table before you go down: people get cut off there every summer, and [this explains why](<ref:page:how-tides-work#h:Reading a tide table> "and how to read the table"), down to [the rule of twelfths](ref:page:how-tides-work#:~:text=twelfths).
- **Robin Hood's Bay**, six miles south: the steep street down to the sea, and the cottage where Nana June lived.
- **Sandsend**, three miles north along the sands at low tide. The walk there along the beach is the best hour you can spend in Whitby.
- **Staithes**, further north again, for the harbour and the painters.

## When to come

Not the August bank holiday, unless you like crowds. Not the goth weekends either, unless you like goths, which I do. October is best: the light's low, the sea's lively, and you can get a table for fish and chips.

## Eating

Fish and chips, obviously, eaten on the harbour wall while the gulls watch you. Hold on to them. The gulls in Whitby are professionals.
