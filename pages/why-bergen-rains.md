Bergen is famous for rain the way Whitby is famous for kippers: everyone has heard, and the real thing is somehow more than you expected. In my first year in Sandviken I kept count, and by my definition, which is enough rain to get your glasses wet, it rained on 241 days.

## The short answer

The wind blows off the Atlantic, full of water, and runs into mountains.

## The longer answer

1. **The wind is westerly.** Bergen is at sixty degrees north, in the belt where the prevailing winds come from the west and south-west, bringing one low-pressure system after another in off the North Atlantic, especially in autumn and winter.
2. **The air is wet.** Those winds have crossed a thousand kilometres of ocean kept warm by the North Atlantic Current, the far end of the Gulf Stream, and warm air holds a great deal of water.
3. **The mountains are in the way.** Bergen sits in a bowl of :hint[seven mountains]{note="Fløyen, Ulriken, Løvstakken, Damsgårdsfjellet, Lyderhorn, Sandviksfjellet and Rundemanen, more or less, depending on who you ask. I've climbed all of them, once in a single day."}, right on the coast, the first high ground the wind meets, and the air is forced up their slopes.
4. **Rising air cools, and cool air can't hold its water.** As it climbs it cools by several degrees for every kilometre, the water condenses into cloud, and the cloud rains out on the city below. It's called :hint[orographic rain]{note="From the Greek for mountain. It's why the windward side of every mountain range near an ocean is wet and the far side dry: by the time the air comes down the other side, it has lost its water."}.

Put together, that's about 2,250 millimetres of rain a year, roughly three times what falls on Edinburgh. Over the mountains to the east, in their shelter, it's much drier: Oslo gets about a third as much.

## Counting it

In October 2022 it rained for [twenty-one days in a row](ref:event:rain-streak "3 to 23 October"). On the third day I wrote an app to count them, as a joke; by the twelfth it had stopped being funny, and [the rain counter](ref:project:rain-counter) is the story of that. The weather station on the kitchen window logged every hour of it.

::file[That October, hour by hour, from the kitchen window]{file="weather-bergen-2022-10" title="Sandviken weather, October 2022"}

The city's record, which every Bergenser can tell you, is :hint[eighty-five days in a row]{note="In the winter of 2006 and 2007. I asked Sverre what he remembered of it. He said: nothing special."}. Twenty-one is a long way from eighty-five. It still felt like a lot.

## What it's like

Not grey, mostly, which surprised me. It's green and silver and very bright between the showers, and the light on the wet stones at Bryggen in the evening is the most beautiful thing in the city. Nobody stops for it. Children walk to school in rain trousers, the fish market opens, the boats go out. Nobody owns an umbrella that has survived a whole winter, because here the wind comes round corners.

Sverre Haldorsen, our old neighbour in Sandviken, a ship's mate for forty years, taught me to read his barometer before I'd learned to read the forecast. When he moved to his daughter's in 2023 he gave it to me. It hangs in the hall at Øvre Lid now, where it rains less, though not much less.

> If the glass falls slowly, the weather stays. If it falls fast, take your washing in.

## A rainy Sunday

On the eleventh day of the streak I gave up on going out at all, made a pot of tea and watched a short film with Biscuit asleep on my feet: a girl, a small dragon, and a great deal of snow. It was exactly the right amount of sad for the weather.
