In August 2018, on Schiehallion, a man a little ahead of us on the boulder field below the top slipped and went down hard, and for about a minute, until he sat up swearing and turned out to be fine, I stood there with my hands open and had no idea what to do. Not the first idea. I had ten Munros and a rucksack full of maps and not one useful thought in my head.

So in March 2019 I spent a weekend learning what to do. Two days, sixteen hours, in a village hall at the foot of the Pentlands and on the hill behind it, with an instructor who had been in mountain rescue for twenty years and a bag of fake blood.

## What it is

Ordinary first aid assumes help is coming in eight minutes. Wilderness first aid assumes it is coming in eight hours, or not until you have gone down to fetch it. So it is less about what to do in the first minute and more about what to do in the first long, cold afternoon: keeping someone warm, splinting with what you have, deciding whether to move them, writing things down, and going for help without making two casualties out of one.

## What stayed

Most of it, I hope. I have not had to use much: a blister on Skye, a cut hand on a spoon-carving course, a woman on the Camino with heat exhaustion whom I sat in the shade and fed salty crisps until she was rude to me, which the instructor had told us was a good sign.

But I am a different person on a hill now. I look at the ground and think about where I would put someone. I carry a bigger bag. And I know that if the man on Schiehallion went down in front of me today, I would have something to do with my hands.
