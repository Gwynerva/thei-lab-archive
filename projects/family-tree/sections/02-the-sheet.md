[The day itself](ref:event:family-tree-sheet). A Saturday in the first lockdown, a sheet of A1 cartridge paper taped to the kitchen table, a dip pen, a bottle of brown ink and a ruler, and a whole day with nowhere to go.

I drew it like a map, because that is the only way I know how to draw anything: the generations as contour lines across the sheet, the oldest at the top, the families as little clusters along each line, the marriages as paths between them. Robin Hood's Bay up in the left corner, Whitby on the right, Staithes in the far top corner with its one dotted line across. Me at the bottom in the middle, a small dot, with Clare beside me and room underneath, in pencil, for whoever comes next.

It took eleven hours. Marta brought me tea at intervals and asked who everyone was, and by the evening knew more about my great-great-grandparents than about her own.

::media[Rooftops at the Bay, where half the people on the sheet were born]{file="robin-hoods-bay-4" layout="centered"}

I photographed it at sunset on the floor, because it would not fit on anything else, and posted the photograph to Nana in a card. She rang on the Thursday to say I had her mother's birthday a week out and a great-aunt's married name wrong, and that it was beautiful, and that I should have it framed. It is framed. It hangs on the landing at Øvre Lid, above the stairs, where I pass it twenty times a day.
