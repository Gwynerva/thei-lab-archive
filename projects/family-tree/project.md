It started on Nana's ninetieth birthday, in June 2019, at a long table in the garden at Robin Hood's Bay, when a cousin of Mum's I had never met asked whether we were related to the Dunns of Staithes, and nobody knew. Nana said, firmly, *certainly not*, and then, less firmly, *well, possibly*. That was enough.

From November 2019 to April 2020 I put the tree together: Nana's side and Grandad Arthur's, back five generations, from me at the bottom to a fisherman born in Robin Hood's Bay in the 1820s at the top. It turned out that we were related to the Dunns of Staithes, distantly, through a marriage in 1868 that Nana had either forgotten or decided to forget.

## What it is

A tree of about ninety people, most of whom lived within twenty miles of Whitby for their whole lives. Fishermen, mostly, on the Dunn side, and the women who baited their lines; then a jet carver, two merchant seamen, a railway porter, a blacksmith; and on Nana's side a long line of women who sewed for a living, which explains a great deal about Nana.

Three things surprised me:

1. How few of them went anywhere. Arthur, who went everywhere, was the exception in two hundred years.
2. How many children did not live. Every family in the 1800s has two or three names in the register that appear once, at a baptism, and again, a year or two later.
3. How often the same names come back: June, three times; Arthur, four. No Mira, until me.

## On one sheet

In April 2020, in the first lockdown, I drew [the whole thing on one sheet](ref:section:family-tree/the-sheet) and posted a photograph of it to Nana. She rang me that evening to correct two dates and to say she had always known about Staithes. It is the last long phone call with her I can remember, and I have it written down in [the diary](/diary/).
