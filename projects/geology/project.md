In April 2020 the world shut and I signed up for everything. Bread, a sweater, a bicycle from a skip, a walk every day, and, on the sixth of April, a sixteen-week online course in geology for beginners from a university that will remain nameless because I have forgotten which one it was.

Why geology: because I had spent a year of my life measuring how fast the cliffs at Whitby were falling into the sea without knowing, really, what they were made of. In the dissertation they were a line on a map, moving. I wanted to know what the line was.

## How it went

A lecture every Monday, a quiz every Thursday, an essay every month, and a rock kit that came in the post: twelve little stones in a box with numbered compartments, which I kept on the windowsill and which Biscuit knocked off one at a time over the summer, as if he were sorting them.

I did the course in the evenings after work and on the daily walk. Edinburgh is the best city in the world for this, because it is sitting on a volcano: Arthur's Seat is what is left of one, and Salisbury Crags are a sheet of hot rock that pushed in between the layers and cooled. For sixteen weeks I walked round the crags with new eyes, poking at things.

The course finished on the thirtieth of July, with [a result](ref:event:geology-done) that I was more pleased with than my MSc. I put the last essay, about the cliffs, into [Whitby's rocks](ref:section:geology/whitby-rocks).

## What I kept

A way of looking. Now I see a road cutting and want to stop the car. I see a cliff and read it from the bottom up, oldest to youngest, the way you would read a diary backwards. And at Øvre Lid, where the hill behind the house is old, hard, grey rock scraped clean by ice, I know now what I am looking at, and it is older than anything in Yorkshire by a very long way.
