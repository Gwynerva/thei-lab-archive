Read from the bottom up, the way the sea reads them.

### The shales

At the bottom, along the beach at low tide, the grey-black shale that the town stands on: mud that settled on the floor of a warm, still sea about a hundred and eighty million years ago, in the Jurassic. It splits into thin leaves like a damp book and crumbles in your hand. This is what the sea eats first, and why the cliffs come down.

### Jet

Inside the shale, here and there, the black glossy stone Whitby is famous for: wood from ancient trees, monkey puzzles, washed into that sea and squeezed for a very long time. The Victorians wore it for mourning. Nana had a brooch of it that had been her mother's, and when I hold it now I think of a tree falling into the sea before there were birds.

### Ammonites

And everywhere, ammonites: coiled shells of animals like squids in snail shells, turned to stone. The common one on the Whitby shore has a name that honours St Hilda, who founded the abbey, because of the legend that she turned the snakes of the headland to stone and threw their heads off the cliff. That is why the coiled stones have no heads, the story goes, and why the town's arms have three of them. Fossil shops used to carve heads on them. Nana's bucket is full of the headless kind.

### The top

Above the shale, sandstone, harder, laid down later by rivers in a delta, which is why the abbey stands on a cap of it and has not yet gone over. And on the very top, a jumble of clay and boulders dumped by the ice that covered all of this twenty thousand years ago, which is the soft part, the part that slumps in a wet winter, the part I measured for my dissertation.

---

So when the cliff falls, it falls from the bottom and the top at once: the sea cutting into the shale below, the rain softening the clay above, and the sandstone in the middle holding on until it can't. I spent a year measuring the rate at which it happens. It took a lockdown to understand why.
