When Nana June died in November 2020 she left a quilt on the frame in her front room at Robin Hood's Bay. The top was finished: a log cabin, in pinks and blues and creams cut from sixty years of other things, dresses and pillowcases and one of Grandad's shirts. Three of the twelve rows were quilted, in her small, even stitches. The other nine were tacked and waiting.

After the funeral Mum folded it into a sheet and put it in my arms. She said, "You're the one who sews." I'm the one who sews. Dad drove me back to Leith with it, and with her sewing box, in the boot of his car.

## A year of stitching

I quilted the rest by hand, through 2021, a row a month more or less. In Leith, through the second lockdown, with the quilt over my knees and the radiator on. Then in a box on a ship to Bergen. Then in the flat in Sandviken, on the sofa, with the rain on the window and Biscuit asleep on whichever bit I needed next.

My stitches are bigger than hers. You can see exactly where she stopped and I started, in the fourth row, a third of the way along. I thought about unpicking her last few stitches so the change would fall at the end of a row, and then I thought: no. That's where she stopped. That's part of it.

> A stitch in time is still a stitch.

That's what she said. About everything: about mending, about phoning your mother, about starting things late.

I finished it on the 21st of November 2021, a year to the day, in the evening, with a candle on the table. I didn't plan the date. Or I did, but only in the way you plan things you can't admit to planning.

:::private
I cried more finishing it than I did at the funeral. At the funeral there were twenty people and a cold chapel and Mum to look after. With the quilt it was just me and her stitches and mine.
:::

It's on our bed now. In winter Biscuit sleeps on Nana's rows and never mine, which I've decided is a compliment to her.
