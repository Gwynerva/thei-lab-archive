This is the cello I learned on at school in Whitby, a factory instrument from the seventies with a scratch on its lower bout shaped like Norway. I stopped lessons at fourteen and stopped pretending at sixteen, and for nine years it lived in its case in Mum and Dad's loft. At Christmas 2017 I carried it down the loft ladder, took it back to Edinburgh on the train, and in January 2018, at twenty-five, I had my first lesson in twelve years.

Since then: three years of lessons with Mrs Ogilvie, the Leith Scratch Orchestra, a lockdown spent alone with Bach, a move across the North Sea, an audition at thirty, the Sandviken Strykeorkester, and in April 2026 a recital in a church, in which I played the prelude and the two minuets of the first suite in front of about eighty people and survived.

## Why the cello

Because I missed having a sound that was mine.

That is the short answer and it is true. The longer one goes back to the music room at school, which smelt of rosin and radiators, and to being eleven and given the biggest instrument in the cupboard because I was tall and nobody else wanted it. I loved it at once, the way you love a big dog. You do not hold a cello; you put your arms round it, and it hums against your chest, and every note you play you feel before you hear.

I gave it up for the usual reasons: exams, being sixteen, a teacher who sighed. I did not miss it for years. Then in my first year of work I sat at a desk all day making maps of water nobody would ever see, and I started to want something that used my hands and my breath and not my eyes, and I kept thinking of that hum.

:::quote[Mrs Ogilvie, at the first lesson, January 2018]{align="center"}
The bow is the breath. The left hand is only the words.
:::

It is also, I have found, the instrument for people who like to be in the middle of things without being looked at. Cellos sit in the middle of the orchestra and hold the floor up. Nobody watches the cellos. I like that more than I can say.

## Now

Rehearsals every other Tuesday in Sandviken, which since the move is two and a half hours each way by bus and ferry, and worth every minute. Scales in the mornings while the kettle boils. Biscuit leaves the room at about minute four, as he has since 2019, and comes back when I stop, as if to say: well done, that's over.
