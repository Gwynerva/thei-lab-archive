Øvre Lid stands about a hundred and forty metres above the fjord, on the last flat ground before the hillside turns into birch wood and then into rock. It faces south-west, which is why there are apples. It was built in 1891 of timber on a stone cellar: two storeys and a loft, white boards outside, painted panels inside in the colours of whatever paint was cheap that decade. A green, a blue-grey, and a yellow that Jonas calls *egg* and I call *custard*.

Downstairs: the kitchen, the *stova*, and a small room behind the kitchen that has been a bedroom, a dairy and a place for boxes, in that order. Upstairs: three bedrooms under sloping ceilings and a landing wide enough for a desk, which is where my desk is. Above that the loft, which is dark, dry and full of Ragnhild's father's tools.

## Fourteen windows

I counted them on the day of the viewing and I count them still, the way a child counts stairs:

1. The south wall, facing the fjord: six. Two in the kitchen, two in the *stova*, two above in the big bedroom.
2. The east wall: three. The small room, the landing, the stairs.
3. The west wall: two, both in the *stova*, for the evening light.
4. The north wall: one, a little one in the pantry, which is cold on purpose.
5. The gables: two, one at each end of the loft, the eastern one looking straight at Folgefonna.

Six and three and two and one and two. Each has two casements of three panes, except the pantry window, which is smaller and has opinions. The glass is old enough to be wavy: the fjord through it moves when you move your head.

## The barn

Across the yard and uphill, so that the hay could be wheeled in at the top and forked down at the bottom. Log walls on a footing of dry stone, a ramp of stone and a bridge of planks up to the big doors of the loft, and a low byre underneath where Ragnhild's family kept four cows and later nothing. Ragnhild thinks the walls are older than the house, carried up here log by log from a farm lower down; the roof is younger, rebuilt in 1931 after a winter took the old one. The logs are dovetailed at the corners and the gaps are stuffed with moss. It is bigger than the house, and when we came there was still hay in the loft.

The roof is the trouble again. The north end has let the sky in for years, and the floor of the loft is soft where the rain falls. It is [the next job](ref:section:farmhouse/now).
