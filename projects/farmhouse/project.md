The house is called Øvre Lid, which means something like *the upper slope*, and that is what it is: a white wooden farmhouse of 1891 on a shelf of land above Utne, the Hardangerfjord below it and Folgefonna across the water, white in every month of the year. It has fourteen windows, a barn with a bridge up to the loft, a well, a woodshed that leans to the east, and an orchard of forty trees that were here long before us and, if we do this properly, will be here long after.

Jonas and I have lived here since the end of March 2025. Before us it was Ragnhild's for most of a century, before Ragnhild her parents', and before them it belonged to a man called Lars who built it and cut his initials into the beam over the cellar door. I keep this page for whoever finds it, and for myself in ten years, when I will have forgotten what the kitchen looked like with no kitchen in it.

## How we came to it

Ragnhild is Jonas's great-aunt. She was born in this house in 1934 and lived in it until the spring of 2024, when the stairs got the better of her and she moved to the care home in Odda. She would not sell it to a stranger and she would not leave it empty, because, she said, *a house that stands empty goes back to the hill*. In August she gave Jonas the key in an envelope, and Jonas, being Jonas, put it in a drawer and said he would have to ask me.

We drove over on a grey Saturday in November to look ([the viewing](ref:section:farmhouse/viewing "Sigrid said she knew the turning")). I had been in Hardanger before, in blossom time, the spring I first met Sigrid, but I had never been up that track. The house had been shut since the spring and smelled of cold wood and apples. I walked round it twice, counting windows, came back down to Jonas in the yard and said "fourteen", as if that settled something. It did, in a way.

We moved in at the end of March, with the snow still lying in the shadow of the barn. The first night we slept on a mattress on the floor of the *stova*, the room with the two west windows, in our hats, because the chimney man had said no fire until he had been, and I lay awake listening to a house I did not know yet: the boards ticking as they cooled, something in the loft that was probably a mouse, the wind finding every gap in fourteen old windows. At about four Biscuit came out from under the blankets and lay across my feet, which he had not done since Leith, and I took that as his vote.

The arrangement is not a mortgage and not quite a gift: we pay Ragnhild rent for now, and if we stay, and we will stay, there is a price, already agreed, which she says is for the trees and not the house. The numbers live in a spreadsheet and on [a page nobody else reads](ref:section:farmhouse/costs), and I try to let them live there and not in my head at three in the morning.

:::private
They live in my head at three in the morning anyway. Freelance money comes in like weather: some months a river, some months a dry bed. Jonas says the house will not run away. I know. I still look at the account every Friday, and I have not told Mum what the stove cost.
:::

## A year and a half in a list

Renovation is the one thing I still keep in checklists. This is the long one, more or less in order:

- [x] Move in with no kitchen, March 2025
- [x] A man on the roof, then a man with tiles
- [x] Test the well
- [x] Take out, mend and put back fourteen windows
- [x] Insulate the north wall
- [x] Light the stove
- [x] Take out the old kitchen and put in a new one
- [ ] The barn roof
- [ ] The barn floor
- [ ] Paint the house, white, because Ragnhild says so and she is right
- [ ] Prop up the woodshed before it lies down for good

Every ticked line has its own page below. The list is here to remind me that there were seven of them, that they took a year, and that a year is not long for a house that has stood for a hundred and thirty-five.

## The windows, briefly

What the windows are, all fourteen, is in [the house](<ref:section:farmhouse/the-house#h:Fourteen windows> "one by one"); how we mended them is in [the windows](ref:section:farmhouse/windows). Here only this. I had never done anything with glass before. By window five I could get the old putty out in one long curl if the day was warm. By window ten I was talking to them. Window fourteen, the small one in the gable that looks straight at the glacier, went back in on the twenty-ninth of August, and Jonas brought two glasses of Sigrid's cider up the ladder, and we drank them sitting on the scaffold boards with our legs hanging over the orchard.

Dad came in July with Mum, under orders from his cardiologist to rest, and rested by measuring the tide at the bottom of the orchard and, when nobody was looking, by sitting at the trestles in the barn with a putty knife. He did not say much. He did say, holding a pane up to the light, that the glass was older than the frames, which meant somebody had mended these before, carefully, a long time ago. I think about that more than I can explain.

## The stove

The best room in an old Hardanger house is the :hint[stova]{note="the best room of an old farmhouse, kept for visitors, Christmas and laying out the dead"}, and ours had a stove in it that nobody would let us light. The chimney sweep looked up the flue in May, made a noise I will remember for the rest of my life, and wrote us a report in which the word *ikke* appeared eleven times. So: a new steel liner down the old chimney, and a second-hand cast-iron stove from a farm sale in Kinsarvik, black, heavy, with a little door you open with a hook.

We lit it on the eighteenth of October. It was the first evening the house felt as if it were on our side.

## The kitchen

When we came there was a kitchen in the sense that there was a room with a sink in it, a cooker that had not cooked since about 2009, and an old black range in the corner that nobody dared light. The sink drained into a bucket. For the first summer we cooked on a camping stove on the porch and washed up in the yard, which is lovely in June and less lovely in October. In November we took everything out but the range, down to the floorboards; in March, with birch cupboards Jonas built like a boat, an oak worktop and an electrician who whistled the whole of his last day, it became a kitchen, and the range, blacked and mended, became the warmest place in it. The five months in between are in [the kitchen](ref:section:farmhouse/kitchen), mostly as lists, because that is how I survived them.

## Who else lives here

### Biscuit

Biscuit is about ten now, and slower, and has decided that the barn is his office. He sits in the doorway of the byre in the afternoons and supervises the orchard. He went missing for a night in our first June and came back in the morning with a burr in his ear and no explanation. I did not sleep. He did.

### Everybody else

Swallows in the barn from May, under the loft floor, who shout at Biscuit and are right to. A hare in the orchard at dusk. Mice in the loft, whom Biscuit ignores on principle. Eirik and Kari below us, with the cider press, a few sheep and a dog who visits us for a second breakfast. The fruit-farm tractors on the road at six in the morning in September. The ferry, which we can hear from the landing if the wind is right, and which I have started to time my coffee by.

## What the house is teaching me

Nana used to say:

> Make do and mend.

I always heard it as a saying about being poor. Living here, I hear it differently: it is a saying about attention. You cannot mend a thing you have not looked at properly, and this house wants looking at. The way the floor of the *stova* slopes towards the fjord. The beam with Lars's initials. The calendar from 1987 still on the back of the pantry door, with the days the ferry did not run crossed out, and on the door frame beside it, in pencil, the day of the first snow for every winter Ragnhild kept count. I copied them all into a notebook before we touched that frame. 1961, the third of November. 2023, the twenty-eighth of October. Sixty-three winters in pencil.

Ragnhild, when I rang her in Odda to ask whether we were allowed to take the old putty out, said this, and I wrote it on the inside of a cupboard door in the new kitchen where only we will see it:

:::quote[Ragnhild, on the telephone from Odda, June 2025]{align="center"}
They were never straight. Don't make them straight.
:::

Sigrid, Jonas's mother, who has firm opinions about everything that grows or rots, said the house would be fine and that we should leave the orchard alone for one year and watch it. We did. That is [another page](ref:project:cider).

## Next

The barn. It is older than the house, by Ragnhild's reckoning: a log barn on a stone footing with a bridge of planks up to the loft, and its roof has been letting the sky in at the north end for at least five winters. The roofer comes [the day after tomorrow](ref:diary:today+2). Until then it is a place for Biscuit to sit and look important, and for me to stand in the half-dark with a torch and plan.

The barn is the last big thing. After that, Jonas says, it is only maintenance for the rest of our lives, and he says it as though that were good news. I have come round to thinking it is.
