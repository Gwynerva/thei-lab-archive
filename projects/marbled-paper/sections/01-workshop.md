Ten till four in a print studio up a close off the Royal Mile, eight of us round two steel trays, and a teacher who had been marbling for thirty years and still said "oh!" every time she lifted a sheet.

The first hour was all theory, which I loved and the man next to me did not. The size has to sit overnight. The colours have to be tested one drop at a time, because each spreads differently: a drop of ochre opens into a circle the size of a coin, a drop of blue the size of a saucer, and if you get the balance wrong one colour eats the others. "Colour," she said, "is a negotiation."

Then we made stone patterns (drop, drop, drop, lay the paper, lift), then a combed one, pulling a comb of pins across the tray and back, and then a nonpareil, which is the combed one done again with a finer comb until it looks like fabric.

I came home with eleven sheets, blue fingers and a carrier bag of second-hand combs she let us buy for a pound each. Priya asked if I'd been dyeing something. I said, "Paper," and she said, "Of course you have."
