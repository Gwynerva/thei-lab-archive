In July 2019 I walked the Cleveland Way with Dad, from Saltburn to Filey, and every evening in a different guest house I found myself drawing the day's coast from memory and getting it wrong in the same places. So when I got home I went looking for how other people had drawn it, over three hundred years, and I didn't stop until Christmas.

## What I collected

Everything I could find that showed the coast between Staithes and Ravenscar, at any scale, from public collections:

- the large-scale Ordnance Survey sheets, from the first one in 1853 onwards
- Admiralty and older sea charts, with the cliffs drawn in profile for sailors to recognise
- county maps of the eighteenth century, beautiful and vague
- a hand-coloured geological map of the coast from a book of the 1820s
- estate plans of farms above Robin Hood's Bay, one of them in [the county archive](ref:section:old-maps-yorkshire/county-archive)

## Why

Partly the dissertation, two years on: I'd used seven of these maps [to measure the cliff](ref:project:whitby-erosion) and never had time to look at them properly. Partly because I'd started to think of maps as letters from the people who drew them, and these were letters about home.

And partly Nana. On the first six-inch sheet, surveyed around 1850, you can find her lane in Robin Hood's Bay, and the cottage, and a dot that might be the rowan tree or might be a well. For Christmas 2019 I framed that corner of the sheet and gave it to her. She held it at arm's length and then very close, and then she said:

> They've got the garden wall wrong. It went round, not across.

It was our last Christmas together. The frame hung in her kitchen for eleven months, and it hangs in mine now, with the wall still wrong.
