In February 2021, three months after Nana died, a parcel came to the flat in Leith from Dad. Inside, beside Nana's sewing box, was a toffee tin with a stagecoach in the snow on its lid, and inside the tin were letters: forty-one of them, in bundles by year, tied with sewing thread.

They are the letters Nana June and my grandad Arthur wrote to each other between 1952 and 1959, while he was at sea. His to her she kept in the tin. Hers to him he kept in his sea-chest, and when he came ashore for good he put them in the tin too, so that the two halves of every conversation ended up together in a cupboard in Robin Hood's Bay, where they sat for sixty years.

I never knew Arthur. He died in 1998, when I was six, and I remember a big quiet man with a pipe he was not allowed to light indoors. In the letters he is twenty-six, and funny, and homesick, and very bad at spelling, and very good at describing the sea.

## What I did with them

I read them all, in order, over a fortnight, at the kitchen table in Leith, in the second lockdown. Then, because I am who I am, I typed them up: [two winters of transcribing](ref:section:nana-letters/transcribing), one letter at a time, keeping every spelling and every crossing-out. And for Christmas 2022, in Whitby, I gave Mum [a book of them](ref:section:nana-letters/for-mum), bound by hand, with a scrap of Nana's quilt fabric on the cover.

Not every page of them is in the book. [Some I kept back](ref:section:nana-letters/kept), and that section is private, because some things in a marriage are not for the grandchildren, even when the grandchildren have read them.

## Why it matters to me

Because it is where my name comes from. In 1952 June sailed with Arthur once, on a short run from Hull to Lisbon and back that the company allowed wives on, and one night on the deck he showed her a star in the constellation of the Whale, a star that brightens and fades over most of a year and that sailors called the wonderful one. She wrote about it for the rest of the decade. Forty years later Mum named me after it.

And because the letters gave me back a Nana I did not know: not the small sharp woman with the rowan jelly and the sayings, but a girl of twenty-two on her own in a cottage up a steep lane, with a husband on the other side of the world, writing to him every Sunday night by the light of the fire, telling him about the weather. [Robin Hood's Bay](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Robin_Hood%27s_Bay "the steep lane, the rowan tree, the beach at low tide") in the 1950s, through her eyes, is the best thing I have ever read.
