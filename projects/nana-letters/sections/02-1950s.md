---
title: The 1950s
summary: What the letters say, and what they don't
---

What they write about, mostly, is weather. His weather and hers: a gale off the Cape, fog in the Channel, a week of rain in the Bay that brought the cliff down on to the beach by the slipway. I come from a long line of people who think the weather is news.

And money, carefully. And her mother's legs. And the price of coal. And which neighbour said what at chapel. And his ship, every ship: the food, the cook, the engineer who played the mouth organ, the dolphins off Ceylon. He writes like he talks, Mum says, which is to say not much and then suddenly a great deal.

:::quote[Arthur to June, November 1955, from the Bay of Biscay]{align="center"}
I looked for your star tonight and found it, low over the stern. It is getting faint again. The wonderful one: it comes and goes, but it always comes back. Like me.
:::

Hers are longer. She writes every Sunday night, four sides of thin blue paper, and you can hear the fire and the clock. She tells him what she has sewn that week, down to the buttons. She never once says she is lonely, except that every letter ends with a count:

> Thirty-one Sundays since you went. Twenty-one to go, if the company keeps its word, which it won't.

What they do not write about: anything that frightened either of them, until 1957. That is the year of the pages I kept back.

Reading them, I recognised so much of myself that it made me laugh out loud in the empty kitchen: the counting, the weather, the lists, the way she makes a small map of the garden in the margin of a letter to show him where she has put the new rhubarb. I thought I had invented drawing maps in the margins. I had inherited it.
