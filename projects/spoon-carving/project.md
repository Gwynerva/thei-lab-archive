I signed up for a spoon carving weekend in October 2021 because I'd been in Bergen two months, it had rained for most of them, and I needed something to do with my hands that wasn't unpacking. I'd expected whittling: a penknife, a stick, a campfire. It was nothing like that.

The [course](ref:event:spoon-course "a weekend in October, in a barn") was in a barn on a farm north of the city, run by a man with a beard like a hedge and hands like tree roots, who had been carving spoons for forty years and spoke slowly in English for me and quickly in Norwegian for everyone else. There were six of us. On the first morning he gave us each an axe, a Mora knife with a red handle, a hook knife and a green birch log as thick as my arm, and said, "Now we find the spoon in there."

## Green wood

The secret is that you carve the wood wet, the week it's cut, or nearly. Green birch is soft and kind and cuts like cold butter; dry birch is like carving a radiator. You split the log with the axe, rough out the shape with the axe, then refine it with the knife, using grips with names like the thumb push and the chest lever, which sound like wrestling moves and feel like them too. Then the bowl with the hook knife, curling out shavings like apple peel. Then you let it dry for a week and go over it again.

## What it gave me

Eleven spoons over the winter, carved at the kitchen table in Sandviken on newspaper, from birch logs I begged from a neighbour who was clearing his garden. A Norwegian word, *sløyd*, for the old idea that making things by hand makes a person better. A reason to learn the words for *knife*, *axe* and *splinter* before the words for *invoice* and *tax return*.

And, as it turned out, a way into the next thing. Three months after the course I met a man who builds boats, and it turns out that a man who builds boats has strong opinions about knives, and that a woman who has carved eleven spoons is ready to be told she could build [a kayak](ref:project:kayak).
