In March 2023 I bought an accordion at a flea market in a school gym in Bergen, for six hundred kroner, from a man who said it had been his father's and that it "still breathes". It's a piano accordion in red pearloid with cream keys and a chrome grille, made in the 1950s, heavy as a toddler, and it did still breathe, with a little wheeze on the way in.

I wanted it because I'd heard one played at a dance in a barn in Hardanger the summer before, and the whole room had stood up. I wanted to be the person who makes a whole room stand up. That's not a good reason to learn an instrument.

## Eight weeks

I tried. A book of beginner tunes from the library, a video course, twenty minutes most evenings, in the flat, with the window shut and Biscuit under the bed. The right hand is a piano, more or less, and I can find my way round a piano. The left hand is a grid of a hundred and twenty buttons you can't see, laid out in a pattern called the circle of fifths, and my left hand, which has spent years on a cello fingerboard, simply refused.

The bellows were the worst. You push and pull, and the music comes in the push and the pull, and if you change direction in the wrong place the tune hiccups. Mine hiccupped. All the time.

## Giving up

By May I'd learned three tunes, badly, and I'd stopped looking forward to the twenty minutes. It was 2023; I wasn't looking forward to much. The cello was there in the corner the whole time, and in May I picked it up instead and played the Bach prelude I'd learned in lockdown, and it was like coming home after a holiday that went wrong.

There's more on [why the cello](<ref:project:cello#h:Why the cello> "and why it stuck") elsewhere. The accordion went to a girl of twelve who answered my advert. Her mother said she'd been asking for one for a year. She played me a tune on the doorstep before they took it away, and it was better than anything I'd managed.
