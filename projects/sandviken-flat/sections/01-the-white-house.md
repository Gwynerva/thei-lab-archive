Number something on a lane so steep that the pavement has steps in it. White boards, a red tile roof, green window frames, a little garden in front with a rowan tree, which I took as a sign, and a back yard with a bike shed with a tin roof, which I later recorded in the rain.

Inside, on the first floor: a kitchen with a window over the harbour, a living room with a wood stove we were not allowed to use, a bedroom under the eaves where you could only stand up in the middle, and floors that sloped so much that a marble put down by the door rolled all the way to the window. I tested this. So did Biscuit.

Sverre lived on the ground floor. He was eighty when I came, a retired ship's mate who had sailed on the coastal steamer for thirty years, and he knew Bergen weather the way Dad knows Whitby tides. Every morning he tapped the brass barometer in his hall and told me what it was going to do, in Bergen Norwegian, slowly, until I understood. He was always right. He said the barometer was always right and he just read it out.

::media[A barometer like his, brass, with the weather words round the dial]{file="barometer-2" layout="centered"}

In the summer of 2023 he moved to his daughter's in Os, because of the stairs. On his last morning he took the barometer off the wall, wrapped it in a newspaper and gave it to me, and said that now I would have to read it out myself. It hangs by our door at Øvre Lid. I tap it every morning. It says rain.
