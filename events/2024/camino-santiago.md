## The last morning

Padrón to Santiago is twenty-five kilometres, and I walked the last of them as slowly as I could. Out at six, in fog, past the stone poet under his great tree, through eucalyptus woods that smelled of cough sweets, along lanes where the yellow arrows came so often they stopped being directions and became company.

I'd walked most of it alone since Viana. Not lonely. Alone the way a kayak is alone on a flat sea: everything around you and nothing asking.

The days since the Minho had a shape I'll miss. Up at six, coffee standing at a bar, walk till two, wash the socks, find the stamp, eat whatever the menu of the day was, sleep in a room full of strangers breathing. Baiona and its harbour full of yachts. The long grey edge of Vigo. Pines, and vines on granite posts, and a *hórreo* in every garden like a small stone chapel for maize. Every evening a little map in the notebook of where I'd been, and nothing in it about where I was going.

## The square

You come into Santiago through suburbs, which nobody warns you about, then the old town, then a passage under the archbishop's palace with a piper playing in it, and then the Obradoiro opens out in front of you all at once: enormous, the towers, the double stairs, people lying on the stones with their packs for pillows.

I lay down too. An hour. Looking up at the towers against the sky, boots off, the woman from Galway I'd met in Baiona lying a few metres off doing the same, neither of us saying anything.

Then I went in and queued to embrace the apostle, which you do from behind, up a narrow stair, with your arms round a pair of gilded shoulders. Then, because I didn't know what else to do with myself, I found the pilgrims' office and queued again.

## What I came home with

The *Compostela*, in Latin, with my name turned Latin: *Miram*. Fifteen days, 280 kilometres, and [the stages](<ref:project:camino#h:Stages> "all fifteen of them") listed in my notebook with a small map of each one in the margin.

I didn't find anything on the Camino. I don't think that's what it's for. I put something down, somewhere around Vigo, and didn't go back for it.

Nana would have said I'd worn out a good pair of boots for nothing. Then she'd have asked to see the map.
