I saved Ben Nevis. Everyone told me to do it first and get it out of the way, so obviously I didn't. Four years of Munros, and the highest was always going to be the last, whenever the last turned out to be. It turned out to be today, four weeks before I leave Scotland.

## Getting there

Callum drove up from Glasgow on Friday night with a bag of rolls from The Wee Oven and a flask the size of a fire extinguisher. Marta came too, though she says one mountain a year is plenty and this was it. We slept in a hostel in Fort William, in a bunk room full of people comparing boots.

## Going up

We started from the visitor centre at seven, on the Mountain Track that everyone calls the tourist path, as if that makes it short. It isn't. It zigzags up and up above Glen Nevis, past the lochan at the halfway, then into long stony switchbacks where people stop talking.

The knee held. I've done the physio exercises every day since September like a model patient, and I strapped it anyway, and on the steep bits I counted steps in Norwegian to keep my mind off it. *En, to, tre, fire.* I got as far as *nitti* a few times.

At about a thousand metres we walked into the cloud, and it never let us out.

## The top

The summit plateau in fog is a strange place: grey stones, grey air, cairns appearing one at a time out of nothing, then the ruin of the old observatory and the trig point, and people standing around it in every kind of waterproof. You can't see a thing. You can't see the North Face drop away a few metres from your boots, which is probably for the best.

::media[The cairn coming out of the fog, the only view we got]{file="ben-nevis-3"}

I touched the trig point and said "thirty-four" out loud. Callum handed me a roll with something in it that might have been haggis, and Marta took a photo of the three of us looking damp and delighted.

Thirty-four of 282. That leaves 248 for another life, or another visit.

## Coming down

Coming down was the hard part, as it always is. We came out of the cloud at about the same place we'd gone into it, and suddenly there was the whole of Glen Nevis below us, green and steaming, and Loch Linnhe shining, and the sea beyond.

We had chips in Fort William and drove home through Glen Coe, and I fell asleep against the window before Rannoch Moor.

I'm not ticking off any more for a while. [The Munro map](ref:project:munro-map) has gone a satisfying shade of green, and the next hills I climb will have Norwegian names. There are only seven of them, to start with.
