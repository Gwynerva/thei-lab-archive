The week the dissertation stopped being an idea and became a hard drive full of photos. Sam Okafor drove the department van down with the drone, I came on the train with a rucksack full of batteries, and Dad met us both at the station like a man meeting royalty.

## The plan

1. Ground control points: twenty painted targets along the cliff top, each one fixed with the GNSS receiver to a couple of centimetres.
2. Fly the cliff at low tide, overlapping photos, three passes.
3. Turn the photos into a point cloud back in Edinburgh.
4. Compare it with the old maps and the aerial photos.

## Monday and Tuesday

Wind. Far too much wind to fly. We laid out the targets instead, which meant crawling along the cliff top with a can of spray paint and a pole while walkers asked if we were from the council. I said yes once, to see what would happen. A woman complained to me about dog bins for ten minutes.

## The drone

Wednesday came up grey and still, and Sam had the drone in the air by seven. I'd never seen a drone survey for real. It goes up with a whine like a wasp, finds its line, and then just works, back and forth along the cliff, clicking every second. Sam stood with the controller looking bored, which I've learned means it's going well.

## Thursday

One more flight at the morning low tide, to fill a gap where the drone had lost its signal behind the Nab, and then we packed. Mum fed us both a fry-up the size of a dinner table. Dad helped us load the van and asked Sam so many questions about the batteries that we left Whitby an hour late, and I didn't mind at all.

## What we brought home

- 4,212 photos
- twenty ground control points, all good
- one sunburnt nose (Sam's) and one windburnt face (mine)
- half a tin of Mum's flapjack

Sam says the point cloud will take the lab computer two days to build. I'll sit next to it like it's an egg.
