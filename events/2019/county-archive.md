A day's leave and the early train south to the county record office. I'd ordered the documents in advance, like a proper researcher, and they were waiting on a trolley: three estate plans, a bundle of sea charts, and the big one, [the 1853 survey](ref:section:old-maps-yorkshire/the-1853-survey) of the coast between Whitby and the Bay.

No white gloves, to my disappointment. Clean dry hands, pencil only, foam wedges for the volumes to rest on. The archivist showed me how to hold a map flat with little snakes of lead shot sewn into cloth, and I want some for my desk.

The 1853 survey is beautiful. Hachures for the cliffs, every field named, the high-water line drawn in a fine, wavering hand. I measured from the cliff edge to a field corner on the old sheet and on the new one, and the difference, even allowing for the old surveyor's errors, is the dissertation all over again: the coast is walking inland about a third of a metre a year.

::file[The history of Whitby, 1817, for its chapter on the cliffs]{file="doc-whitby" title="A history of Whitby"}

I photographed what they'd let me, filled half a notebook, and missed the train back by four minutes because I was reading the margin notes on an estate plan about a drowned cow.
