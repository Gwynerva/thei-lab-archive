Three days at a conference on flood mapping: my first abroad, and my first talk to a room that wasn't the Leith meetup. Dougie sent me because, he said, "you wrote the tools, you can explain them". Ines helped me cut the slides from forty to fourteen.

## The talk

I practised it eleven times in the hotel room on Wednesday night: once to the mirror, ten times to the kettle.

Thursday, twenty minutes, a room of about a hundred and twenty people, most of them Dutch, which is a bit like giving a talk about bread to a room full of bakers. I said three things:

:::list{counter="lower-roman"}
1. A flood map for a council isn't for engineers. It's for the person deciding where the sandbags go at two in the morning, and it has to work printed in black and white.
2. Surface water, the rain that never reaches a river, floods more homes in Scotland than the rivers do, and nobody draws it well.
3. Our model is good, but only as good as the drains it doesn't know about.
:::

::media[One of the maps I showed: three return periods, three panels]{file="flood-map-2"}

Ten minutes of questions. One man asked about the drains; I'd hoped somebody would. Afterwards three people asked for the slides, and one of them for the code. Dougie rang that evening to ask how it had gone. I said fine. He said, "Fine is good. Fine is what we do."

## The rest

Canals at blue hour, bicycles in heaps, and a whole city sitting below the sea and perfectly calm about it.

I walked everywhere, of course. On the first morning I got up early and walked the canal ring from end to end with a paper map, and found it oddly familiar: the canals are drawn in rings like contour lines, as if the whole city were a hill somebody had turned upside down and filled with water. On the Singel a man was fishing a bicycle out of the canal with a hook on a rope. Nobody stopped to watch. It's that normal.

On the last evening the organisers projected a short film onto the wall of a bar by the water, made right here in Amsterdam a few years ago, robots and all, as a joke about what happens when nobody reads the flood maps.

::embed{url="https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=R6MlUcmOul8"}

Flew home on Friday night with a tote bag, a cold, and the feeling that I might, possibly, know what I'm talking about.
