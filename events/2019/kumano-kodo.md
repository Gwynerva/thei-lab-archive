I came to Japan for this. Four days on the [Kumano Kodo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kumano_Kod%C5%8D "a thousand years of pilgrims"), the Nakahechi route, from Takijiri to the Grand Shrine at Hongū: about forty kilometres through the mountains of the Kii peninsula, my bag sent on ahead each morning, and a little stamp book to fill.

::media[Where I was: the south of the Kii peninsula]{file="map-kumano"}

## Day one: Takijiri to Takahara

The shortest day and the steepest. You start at a shrine by a river and go straight up through cedar roots, which are the stairs. Takahara is a village on a ledge, and from the guesthouse balcony I watched cloud fill the valleys below like milk poured into a dish.

## Day two: Takahara to Chikatsuyu

Forest all day. The cedars are enormous and very straight, and the light comes through them in slices. Every so often there's an *oji*, a small wayside shrine with a wooden box and a stamp in it, and I stamped every one like a child with a sticker chart. I met perhaps ten people all day. One of them, an old man going the other way, gave me a mandarin and said something long and kind that I didn't understand a word of.

## Day three: Chikatsuyu to Hosshinmon-oji

Rain. Proper rain, the West Highland kind. The stone steps ran like streams and the moss on everything turned a green you could nearly hear. I sat under the roof of a rest hut eating rice balls and listened to the water for twenty minutes.

::audio[The stream below the rest hut, in the rain]{file="sound-stream-1" title="Stream, day three"}

## Day four: down to Hongū

The last morning was dry and blue. From Hosshinmon-oji it's seven kilometres downhill to the Grand Shrine, through tea fields and villages, and then you come out above the river flats at Ōyunohara and see the great torii standing in a field on its own, the largest in the world and looking it.

The shrine itself is dark wood and bark roofs and white banners, quieter than anything in Kyoto. I got my last stamp and sat on the steps for a long time.

## What I'll remember

- The lightness of not carrying the bag.
- Hot baths every night; the *onsen* at Yunomine smelling of eggs.
- Dinner on a tray, twelve small dishes, half of which I couldn't name.
- Walking alone for four days and never once feeling lonely.

Every evening I drew the day's route in the margin of my notebook: a thin line wriggling east, a bit longer each night. It's the best map I've drawn all year, and it's two inches long.
