Forty-three hours. Two nights, the first calm and the second not, and one morning of fog so thick that Jonas and I took turns at the bow, listening for things.

Land came up at eleven as a low grey line I did not trust for half an hour, then as cliffs, then as Bressay lighthouse, white on its point, exactly where the chart said it would be. I cried a bit, which Jonas politely did not see. We came up Bressay Sound with the town on the left, grey stone and painted gables and boats, and tied up in the harbour at half past one.

::media[Bressay Sound on the 1897 chart I had been reading for a month; the soundings have barely changed]{file="lerwick-2"}

The first thing about landfall is the ground, which moves for a day. I walked up the street to the shop holding on to walls. The second is how much you want a shower. The third is chips, which we had, on the harbour wall, in the sun.

[Lerwick](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lerwick "seven thousand people, and the most welcome town I know") is the most northerly town in Britain, and from the harbour it feels like Bergen's small grey cousin: the same gulls, the same smell of diesel and fish, Norwegian names on half the boats. Someone on the pontoon heard Jonas and said *god dag* before we had said a word.
