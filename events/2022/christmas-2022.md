Christmas at home in Whitby, the first since 2019, the first ever without Nana, and the first with Jonas.

## Getting there

Bergen to Amsterdam to Newcastle, a train to Middlesbrough, then the bus over the moors in the dark, Jonas's knees against the seat in front, the heather black and the sky full of stars. Mum met us in the rain with an umbrella for each of us and the face she makes at the start of term. She hugged me, and then she hugged Jonas, who wasn't ready for it.

## Dad and Jonas

I'd worried. Dad doesn't talk to people he doesn't know, and Jonas doesn't talk much to anyone. I needn't have. On the first morning Dad took him down to the shop "for half an hour", and they came back at four, having been through every boat on the slipway and every rope in stock, and agreed that varnish isn't what it was.

Dad is well. Three months after the stent he's back in the shop every day, and walks to the end of the pier and back every morning on the doctor's orders, complaining all the way, which is how we know he's well.

## The letters

On Christmas Eve, after dinner, I gave Mum the book. All year I've been typing out Nana's letters from the tin: the 1950s ones, from when Grandad was at sea, and the ones she sent me at university. I printed them and bound them by hand in blue cloth, with Fergus's marbled sheet for the endpapers. [The book](ref:section:nana-letters/for-mum "a year of evenings, again") is the best thing I've ever made.

Mum read the first page, and closed it, and held it on her lap and didn't say anything for a long time. Then she said, "She'd have told you the stitching's crooked." It isn't. Then she went to bed early, with the book.

## Christmas Day

Clare and Dev came for Christmas Day, Clare straight off a night shift and asleep on the sofa by three. Crackers, which Jonas had never seen and took very seriously, reading his joke out twice in case he'd missed something. Paper hats. Dad carving. Mum's pudding, set alight, which Jonas also took very seriously.

On Boxing Day we walked along the old railway line to the Bay and looked at Nana's cottage from the lane. Someone has painted the door blue. The rowan tree is still there, bare, with a few berries the birds have missed.

On the last night Dad shook Jonas's hand at the door and said, "You'll do." Jonas asked me later what it meant. I told him it's the highest honour Whitby gives.
