Dad drove up in the van this morning with a coil of mooring rope for a boatyard in Granton, a box of Mum's apples, and, without any warning at all, my cello. "Your mother needs the space under your bed," he said, which is not the reason, and we both knew it.

It's in its old black case with my name in Tipp-Ex on the lid, the way I left it at sixteen. Lifted the lid an inch to check it had survived the van, saw the scratch on the front that I always said was shaped like Norway, and shut it again. It's leaning in the corner of my room now. It's looking at me. I'm not looking back yet.

:::private
Dad carried it up all seventy-two stairs himself and wouldn't let me take it. At the top he had to stand on the landing for a whole minute, pretending to admire the view from the stair window. He's fifty-four. I didn't say anything. I'm writing it down so I can stop thinking about it.
:::

Bacon rolls at the kitchen table, then he drove home the same afternoon. Four hours each way, for rope and apples and a cello.
