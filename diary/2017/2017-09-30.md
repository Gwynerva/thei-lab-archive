## The rowan

Took the train down after work last night: Edinburgh, Darlington, Middlesbrough, then the little line across the moors to Whitby, Dad at the station, and the car up to Robin Hood's Bay in the dark. Nana's cottage up the steep lane, the light on, the kettle already on.

This morning the rowan in her garden was so heavy with berries the branches were bending. Nana is eighty-eight and directed operations from a kitchen chair on the path: "Not those, they're not ready. Those. Mind the bird ones." (The bird ones are the top branches, which she leaves for the blackbirds.) Three pounds of berries, stalks off by hand, both of us with orange fingers.

## The jelly

Berries and a pile of chopped crab apples into her big pan with the water, simmered till soft, then tipped into the jelly bag hung from an upturned stool, dripping into a bowl. I reached to give it a squeeze to hurry it up and Nana nearly had my hand off.

:::quote[Nana June, guarding the jelly bag]{align="center"}
Never squeeze. You can have it quick or you can have it clear. You can't have both, and you'll want clear.
:::

So it drips all night, and tomorrow we boil. She's written the recipe out for me on a card in her slanting hand, with the important bit in capitals:

Fish and chips on the sea wall with her in the evening, both of us in two coats. She told me about the rowan she planted when Grandad Arthur was away at sea, "to keep the house safe", and said it's done its job for sixty years.
