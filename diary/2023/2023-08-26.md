The rock at Herdla.

A perfect day for it, the forecast said, and it was: high pressure, the Hjeltefjord flat and blue, Jonas in his old plastic boat and me in *Nana June*. We drove out to Herdla in the morning, put in from the little beach by the trees, and paddled north round the island and out among the skerries.

I'd planned it all at the kitchen table. Low water at 13.20. I'd marked the drying rocks in pencil. I'd checked it against three tables, as I always do.

At about half one, threading between two skerries, there was a noise under me like a chair dragged across a floor, and she stopped, and slewed, and I was sitting on a rock under ten centimetres of water: a rock I had marked in pencil on a chart on the kitchen table, with a crust of white shells on it.

I got off it. I don't remember how. Jonas came alongside and held my deck lines and said *it's fine, it's fine* in the voice he uses for Biscuit at the vet. There was water in the front hatch, not much. We paddled back to the beach very slowly, pulled her up and turned her over.

A scrape along the keel the length of my forearm, white, and the glass rubbed through to the wood in one place the size of a coin. That's all. Jonas said every boat gets one, that she was lucky and so was I. I sat on the stones and cried as if somebody had died, about a boat, about a scrape the size of a coin.

It wasn't the scrape. I know the tides. I've always known the tides. I knew that rock was there.

::media[The beach where we pulled her out, afterwards, everything looking innocent]{file="herdla-1"}

We ate the sandwiches anyway. Jonas found a bit of green sea glass and gave it to me without a word. It's the first time [she](ref:event:kayak-launch#:~:text=Nana) has been hurt, and it was me.
