She floats.

High water at 11.04 and we had her on the slip below the workshop by half ten: Jonas, me, two of his colleagues who'd been promised cake, Anna with her family, and Sverre, who came down the hill with a little flag on a stick and stood at the top of the slip like a harbourmaster. Mum and Dad on the phone, propped against a fender, the sound on this time.

I'd painted the name on the bow on Tuesday night, small and black, in letters copied from Nana's handwriting on the back of an old photo: *Nana June*. Jonas said a boat should know who she is before she gets wet.

:::quote[Nana June, every time anyone went near the water]{align="center"}
Never trust a calm sea.
:::

It was a calm sea. I didn't trust it. I poured a little of Sigrid's cider over the bow — a boat built in Bergen of wood from Africa by a woman from Yorkshire gets Hardanger cider, not champagne — and the rest went round in paper cups.

Then we walked her down the slip, and the water came up her sides, and she lifted. That's the only word for it. She was heavy on the trestles and on our shoulders for sixteen months, and then she wasn't.

I got in from the jetty without falling in, which I'd been more afraid of than the test. She sat a little low at the stern until I moved my weight forward. She tracks straight. She turns when you lean. I paddled out to the end of the moorings and back, and somebody clapped, and Dad on the phone said, "Well, she's level," which from Dad is a speech.

When everyone had gone I took her out again by myself. Nine o'clock, still light, the fjord like glass. I sat in the middle of it with the paddle across the deck and didn't think about work for an hour, which I didn't know I could still do.
