## Syttende mai

My first 17 May, Norway's national day, which is not like any national day I've seen. No tanks, no speeches you'd notice. Children. The whole day belongs to children.

It starts with drums. At seven in the morning the :hint[buekorps]{note="Bergen's own boys' brigades, more than a hundred and sixty years old, with drums and wooden crossbows"} came down the street under the window: small boys in caps and sashes, marching in step with wooden crossbows on their shoulders, the drummers in front, so serious it made me want to cry. Bergen is the only city that has them. You hear them all spring, practising in the evenings.

## The parade

Then the *barnetog*, the children's parade: every school in the city, every child with a flag, school bands in uniform, banners, and on the pavements everyone in *bunad* — the national costumes, embroidered wool and silver, every valley its own — or in their best clothes. I wore my good coat and felt underdressed next to a baby. Everybody shouts *hurra!* for three hours. You don't stop. The flags are everywhere: in windows, on the buses, stuck in the cakes.

## The rest

Ice cream and hot dogs, the only lunch permitted. The *russ* — school-leavers in red overalls covered in signatures — asleep on the grass by the fish market. Jonas in a dark green jacket from Hardanger, which he called "not quite a *bunad*" and which made his mother, on the phone, very happy.

I used to write three good things at the end of a day. Today I'd need about fifty, so here are three:

1. A boy of about six, drumming with his whole body.
2. An old woman in a *bunad* older than me, saying *gratulerer med dagen* to everyone she passed, including a dog.
3. Shouting *hurra* until I couldn't, and nobody thinking it strange.
