## The pool

Eight of us at a swimming pool on the south side of the city at nine in the morning, eight plastic kayaks lined up on the tiles and the whole place smelling of chlorine and neoprene.

The first thing they teach you is how to fall out. You lean forward, tip over, hang there upside down in the warm blue water for a second that lasts about a week, pull the loop of the *spruttrekk*, and slide out like a letter from an envelope. I did it six times. The fourth time I forgot which way was up and surfaced facing the wrong side of the boat, coughing, to a round of applause from a small boy in the next lane.

Then rescues: someone tips over, you paddle up alongside, they haul themselves up on your bow. Then bracing, which is slapping the water flat with the paddle so you don't go over, and which I'm told I do "like stirring porridge".

## The instructor

That was Jonas. Tall, a beard, endlessly patient, a big laugh that echoes off a swimming-pool roof. He's a boatbuilder in the week — he teaches at a boat workshop across the water — and does the courses at weekends "to see the boats used". At lunch he showed us a paddle he'd made, laminated from strips of light and dark wood, and let me hold it but not use it.

::media[Jonas's paddle, which I was allowed to hold and not allowed to use]{file="kayak-paddle-2"}

I told him my dad has a chandlery on Whitby harbour and that I grew up under boats being mended. He said, "Then you know what the inside of a hull smells like." I do.

## Words

- *kajakk* — kayak, with all the k's
- *padleåre* — a paddle
- *spruttrekk* — the spraydeck, the skirt that keeps the sea out
- *kantre* — to capsize; used a lot today

Tomorrow: the sea. In January. Drysuits, they promise.
