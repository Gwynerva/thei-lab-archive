## The hall

A church hall off Great Junction Street, a tea urn, a piano with a cloth over it and about thirty-five chairs in a horseshoe, with people of every age walking in carrying cases of every size. A trombone. Two flutes. A man of about eighty with a viola and a flask. I was the only person who'd arrived twenty minutes early, apart from Agnes Muir, who runs it, and who shook my hand like she was checking it would hold a bow.

## The cellos

Four of us. On my left, a retired GP who counts out loud under his breath and apologises when he gets it wrong, which is often, and which makes me love him. On my right, a schoolgirl who is much better than me and kind enough to hide it.

## The Elgar

We sight-read the first movement twice. The first time I lost my place in bar nine and came back somewhere near bar thirty. The second time I stayed with it nearly to the end, and there was a moment — eight bars, perhaps — where the whole room was playing the same thing and I was one of the people playing it, and the floor hummed. I haven't felt that since the school orchestra. I'd forgotten that it's the best feeling there is that doesn't involve the sea.

Agnes, at the end: "Cellos, lovely. Cellos, also: count."

In the tea break a violinist from the second desk — Polish, I think, called Marta, with the driest laugh in Leith — asked if the scratch on my cello was meant to be Norway. Nobody has ever got it without being told.

Forty-four minutes in the log, orchestra part, lovely. It counts.
