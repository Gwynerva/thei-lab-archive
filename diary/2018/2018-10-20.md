## The tray

Six of us around a long steel tray at Lowe Bindery, filled with what Fergus calls *size*: water thickened with seaweed until it's like very thin custard. You flick colour onto it from a brush — tiny drops, and they sit on the surface and spread out into circles — and each colour has a little :hint[ox gall]{note="bile from a cow, which makes the colours spread out on the size instead of sinking"} in it, to make it spread. Then more colour inside the first colour, and more, until the tray's full of spots like a leopard that has been in a fight.

::media[Dropping colour onto the size, everybody holding their breath]{file="marbled-paper-1"}

## The comb

Then you drag a stick through it, or a comb with long pins, and the spots stretch into feathers and waves and patterns with names: stone, nonpareil, French curl. Then you lower a sheet of paper onto the surface — gently, from one corner, so no air gets under — and lift it off, and the whole pattern has come with it, every hair-width of it. The first time it works you make a noise. Everybody made the noise.

::media[My best one: a stone pattern in blue and black, with some red and yellow that wandered in]{file="marbled-paper-2"}

## What I learned

1. Size is a living thing and has moods.
2. Too much gall and your colour runs off and hides under the others.
3. Paper goes down from one corner, never all at once, never in a hurry.
4. The fourth sheet is better than the first, and the twelfth sheet is worse than the fourth, because by then you're showing off.

Fergus lent me his copy of an old handbook, which I've found as a scan to keep:

::file[The old handbook Fergus learned from; the pages on combs are the good ones]{file="doc-marbling" title="The whole art of marbling"}

The craft is far older than I thought — [paper marbling](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paper_marbling "older than printing in Europe, it turns out") went from Japan and Persia to the bookbinders of Europe, and Fergus says his own teacher learned it from a man who learned it from somebody in Istanbul. Came home with eleven sheets on the bus, flat on my knees, held like a tray of drinks.
