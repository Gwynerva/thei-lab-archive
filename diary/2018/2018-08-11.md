## Number ten

Schiehallion: the fairy hill of the Caledonians, a perfect cone standing on its own in Perthshire, so neat it looks drawn. Priya and I and — the event of the summer — Callum, who has never been up a Munro in his life and took his first Saturday off in a year to do it before he goes to Glasgow.

The path goes up the long east ridge, good track first and then a mile of grey boulders that tilt when you stand on them; Callum went over them like a man crossing a busy kitchen. At the top the cairn stands in a heap of broken pinkish rock, and we sat on it in the wind and ate his sandwiches, which were the best summit sandwiches in the history of Scotland.

::media[Schiehallion across the fields from the Tay, as neat a cone as anyone could draw]{file="schiehallion-1"}

Height: 1,083 metres.\
Time: five and a half hours, one of them on the top.

## Contours

The best thing about Schiehallion is that it's where contour lines come from, more or less. In 1774 astronomers came up here to weigh the Earth — a pendulum would lean a tiny bit towards a big mountain, and how much it leaned would say how heavy the Earth was — and to work out the mountain's own weight they surveyed it all over and ended up with thousands of heights. One of the mathematicians, to make sense of them, joined up the points of equal height with lines. [The Schiehallion experiment](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Schiehallion_experiment "how they weighed the world from this hill") gave us the Earth's density and the contour line in the same summer.

I stood on the top of it and felt like a pilgrim.
