## Getting there

Mrs Ogilvie lives at the top of a tenement in Marchmont, ninety-one steps up — worse than ours — which I counted on the way down, when I could breathe again. I carried the cello up in its soft case like a sleeping child that weighs more than it should.

## The lesson

She is small and very straight and seventy-something, and she played in an orchestra for thirty-one years, and she listened to an open D, a G major scale and half a minuet I learned at thirteen without saying a single word. Then she said:

> The bow is the breath. The left hand is only the words. You've been talking for twelve years without breathing.

It isn't quite twelve years of nothing: I stopped lessons at fourteen and stopped pretending at sixteen. She didn't need the difference.

She took the bow off me, rebuilt my hand round it finger by finger and gave it back. Then forty minutes of open strings. My right shoulder is on fire. It was the best hour of the year so far, admittedly in a short year.

## For next week

- Open strings, ten minutes a day, slow: four beats to a bow, then eight.
- G major, one octave, no vibrato. ("You haven't earned it.")
- Pencil marks on the fingerboard? "Certainly not."

::media[The cello on Mrs Ogilvie's carpet, bow across the strings, looking a great deal better than it sounds]{file="cello-1"}

The long answer to why I'm doing this at all lives [on the cello's page](<ref:project:cello#h:Why the cello> "the long answer, written later"). The short one: I miss having a sound that's mine.

Three good things:

1. She called my bow hold "not hopeless".
2. Chips on the walk home.
3. Nobody heard that first open D but her.
