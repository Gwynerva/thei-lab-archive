## Saltburn

The early train from Whitby up the Esk valley to Middlesbrough and back out to Saltburn, Dad in his old green waterproof reading the tide table like a newspaper. At Saltburn we went down to the pier on the cliff lift, the little water-powered one that's older than Nana, and stood at the end of the pier and looked south along the coast we were going to walk: a long line of cliffs going away into the haze as far as you could see, and then some.

"Right," said Dad. That was the speech.

## Up and over

The path goes straight up Huntcliff from the beach and then along the top, a railway on one side and the drop to the sea on the other, past a sculpture of a giant charm bracelet hung on the fence, which Dad looked at for a long time and said nothing about. Down into Skinningrove, an old ironstone village in a gully, and up again onto Boulby, the highest cliffs on this side of England, two hundred metres down to the rocks. We sat on the top with our sandwiches and watched fulmars riding the up-draught a few feet away, not flapping, just tilting.

## Staithes

Down at last into Staithes, the village wedged into a crack in the cliff, all steps and alleys and washing lines across the gaps. Fifteen kilometres. The guest house had two single beds and a picture of a cat in a bonnet. At dinner Dad told me the whole history of the harbour wall without being asked, then fell asleep at half nine with the tide table on his chest. I sat in the window and drew the day's map: the pier, the bracelet, the cliffs, and the fulmars as little ticks in the margin.
