## Before breakfast

I woke at five to the sound of the grandmother sweeping the yard, the same long patient strokes as the cleaner at Fushimi Inari, as if everybody in Japan who sweeps learned it from the same teacher. Mist lay in the valley below the inn like milk in a saucer. Breakfast: rice, miso, a grilled fish looking at me, pickles in four colours, and a raw egg I didn't know what to do with until the grandmother mimed breaking it into the rice and stirring. She packed me two rice balls wrapped in leaves for the road and then stood at the gate and waved until the bend. I waved back until the bend as well. We both seemed to think it mattered.

## The gate

Hosshinmon-ōji is the outer gate of Hongū, the beginning of the sacred ground: a dark wooden torii at the top of a flight of steps, quiet, with a small shrine behind it and a stamp in a wooden box. You stamp your own book. I've been stamping mine for four days at every shrine that has a box, and it has become one of the more important things I do; I'd walk an extra kilometre for a good stamp. This one is a circle of characters I can't read around a three-legged crow, the messenger of Kumano, who is supposed to have guided the first emperor through these mountains. A crow that knew the way. Dad would approve of a bird like that.

From the gate to Hongū is seven kilometres and mostly downhill, through small tea gardens and villages, past houses with persimmon trees in the yard and women in sun bonnets working in the fields, who said good morning to me without looking up. There were tiny stalls by the path with no one minding them: oranges in bags, a tin for the coins, everyone trusted. I bought a bag of oranges I couldn't carry and carried it.

## Fushiogami

Then the place I'd read about in Edinburgh and not believed. Fushiogami means something like falling down to worship. The path comes round a shoulder of the hill and there, suddenly, far below in the valley, you see the flat ground by the river where the great shrine used to stand, and an enormous gate at the edge of it. The old pilgrims, a month or more out of Kyoto, saw this and fell on their knees. I didn't fall on my knees. I sat on the bench they've put there, which is the modern version, and ate one of the grandmother's rice balls, and found I was crying a little, for no reason I can give. Four days of walking, I think. Or the rice ball. It was a very good rice ball.

## Hongū

Kumano Hongū Taisha sits at the top of a long flight of stone steps, a hundred and fifty-eight of them, lined with tall white banners with black writing, flapping in the wind like washing. At the top, through a gate, the shrine itself: dark buildings under roofs of cypress bark, thick as thatch and silver-brown, with gold at the ends of the beams. Very quiet. Very clean. Nobody in a hurry. A wedding party came through in white and red while I was there, and everyone stood back and nobody took a photograph, so I didn't either.

By the shrine office there's a black postbox with the crow on it. I posted cards to Mum and Dad, to Nana, to Clare and Dev, and one to the flat addressed to Biscuit, which Marta has promised to read to him.

## The great gate

Down by the river is Ōyunohara, the old site. The shrine stood there on a sandbank between the rivers for centuries, until a flood in 1889 swept most of it away; what was left was rebuilt up on the hill where I'd just been. Now the sandbank is a quiet field of grass and trees, and at the entrance to it stands the biggest torii in the world: more than thirty metres of dark steel, so big that walking under it you feel like a mouse going through a door.

The flood interested me more than it should. I map floods for a living, and here was one that moved a god. I stood on the old site and looked at the rivers, low and clear and innocent today, and at the height of the hill they'd moved it to, and did the sum the shrine's builders must have done in 1889: high enough, and then a bit more. It's the same sum I do for councils in Fife. They did it with their feet and a terrible summer; I do it with a computer and a rainfall table. I liked that a great deal.

## Over the Dainichi-goe

Most people stop at Hongū and take the bus. I'd booked the inn at Yunomine, the hot-spring village over the next hill, and there's an old path to it, the Dainichi-goe: three and a half kilometres, steep up and steep down. So up I went, on legs that had done forty kilometres and wanted me to know it. Halfway up, a little shrine with a stone Buddha wearing a knitted hat. Nana will want to know about this one too. At the top the forest opened for a moment and I could see Hongū behind me, very small already, and the river shining. Then down the other side on stone steps worn into dips by a thousand years of feet, into a village that smelled of eggs.

## Yunomine

Yunomine is one of the oldest hot springs in Japan: a single crooked street along a stream that steams. People boil eggs and sweet potatoes in a pool of the spring water at the side of the road, in little nets, and eat them on the bridge. I boiled an egg. It took twelve minutes and was the best egg of my life, which may have been the walk talking.

In the stream there's a tiny wooden hut over a rock pool, Tsuboyu, the only hot spring in the world, they say, that's part of a World Heritage site. The old pilgrims purified themselves in it before going on to Hongū; I did it in the wrong order, but I don't think anyone was keeping score. You take a ticket with a number and wait your turn, and then you have the hut to yourself for half an hour: a wooden box, a stone pool, hot milky water coming up from below, and the stream rushing past on the other side of the planks. I lay in it until I couldn't tell where my legs ended. The water is supposed to change colour seven times a day. I only saw one, but I was only there for half an hour.

## Four days, added up

- About forty kilometres, Takijiri to Hongū and over the hill.
- Four passes, five if this afternoon's counts, and my legs say it does.
- Twenty-three stamps in the book.
- People met on the path: maybe thirty, of whom I talked to six, of whom two were Australian.
- Rain: twenty minutes on Wednesday, as forecast by the old man with the saw.
- Blisters: none, thanks to the first aid course and its very stern blister lecture.
- Times I thought about work: twice, both by accident.

The man who runs the inn tonight told me at dinner that this road and the Camino in Spain are sister routes, and that if you walk both you're a Dual Pilgrim and get a certificate. He said it as if it were the natural next step. I am not walking hundreds of kilometres across Spain. (I said.)

---

I came to Japan because it was the farthest place I could think of that I was frightened of and wanted to see. I thought I'd be lonely, and I was, for an hour on the first day in Kyoto, and then not again. What I didn't expect was the quiet: four days in the forest with no one to explain myself to. I've never gone so long without saying a sentence about maps. Tonight I'm in a cotton robe in a paper room above a steaming stream, with a body that aches all over in an honest way, and I've drawn the whole road in the margin of this page from memory: Takijiri, the rock, the ridge in the mist, the cedars, the spring, the gate, the bench where I cried over a rice ball, the shrine, the great gate, the hill, the bath. It's the best map I've drawn in a long time, and nobody will ever use it to find anything.

Three good things, though it should be thirty:

1. The grandmother waving at the bend.
2. The great gate over the empty field.
3. The bath, and the stream, and the half hour.
